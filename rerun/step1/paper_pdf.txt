             Do Text-to-Music Models Take Direction?
            An Instrumented Audit of Energy-Instruction
                           Compliance


                                          Conner O. Farrell
                                      CCRMA, Stanford University
                                     conner@ccrma.stanford.edu



                                                 Abstract
            Text-to-music systems are steered almost entirely through natural-language
            prompts, yet whether graded perceptual instructions are actually followed is largely
            unknown. We audit five open text-to-music models (2023–2026) on one well-
            measured perceptual axis: musical energy. A frozen matrix of genres × requested
            levels × phrasings (numeric “energy level L out of 11” vs. a five-word verbal
            ladder), six generations per cell, yields 1,500 thirty-second clips. Each clip is
            scored by three instruments—an interpretable, calibrated arousal model, inte-
            grated loudness (LUFS), and CLAP text–audio similarity—and every analysis is
            repeated on loudness-normalized copies to separate “changed the music” from
            “turned the volume knob.” Because the instrument is a sparse linear model over
            named features, compliance decomposes exactly into loudness, noisiness, and
            tonality—mechanisms, not only scores. We find: (1) numeric instructions do
            nothing measurable (all monotonicity CIs cover zero), while verbal instructions
            move every model (verbal−numeric ∆ρ excludes zero for all five); (2) usable
            dynamic range is nonetheless narrow—median achieved movement between “very
            calm” and “extremely intense” spans 0.1–2.05 points on an 11-point scale; (3) a
            feature-level decomposition attributes compliance mostly to noisiness and tonality
            change rather than loudness (loudness share −2–38%), and the one model with the
            largest loudness share independently loses the most compliance under loudness
            normalization; (4) compliance concentrates inside genre-plausible energy bands;
            and (5) one model’s energy decreases at the top instruction level on all three instru-
            ments. We release prompts, scores, features, and analysis code for full statistical
            reproduction.


1    Introduction
Prompting is the primary interface to modern text-to-music (TTM) systems, and prompts routinely
contain graded perceptual direction: a calm folk track, extremely intense EDM. Whether such direction
is followed—monotonically, with usable range, consistently across genres and phrasings—has, to our
knowledge, not been answered with graded instruction ladders and objective measurement. Existing
adherence evaluation is dominated by a single scalar, CLAP text–audio similarity to the whole prompt
[1], which cannot say which part of a prompt was honored, how much the audio moved per unit of
requested change, or by what acoustic means.
We treat this as a measurement problem. We fix one perceptual axis with unusually good measurement
properties—arousal/energy, the most reliably rated affect dimension in music research [2, 3]—and
deploy an interpretable, calibrated arousal instrument [4] alongside integrated loudness [5] and CLAP
similarity [6] over a frozen prompt matrix. The instrument’s interpretability turns an adherence
score into a discovery: a sparse linear model over named acoustic features, its compliance signal

Contributions. (1) The first graded compliance curves for perceptual energy instructions across five
open TTM models spanning 2023–2026 and three architecture families, with clip-level bootstrap
confidence intervals for monotonicity, dynamic range, and phrasing effects. (2) A two-way mechanism
decomposition—loudness-normalized re-scoring plus an exact feature-level attribution—showing
that measured compliance is mostly musical-content change, quantifying each model’s “volume-
knob” share, and demonstrating that the two methods agree. (3) A genre-plausibility analysis
showing compliance is gated by what a genre palette can plausibly reach, and two top-of-scale
phenomena: instruction saturation (energy falling from level 7 to level 9) and instrument ceiling
effects, disentangled using pre-calibration scores. (4) A fully reproducible release: prompts, per-clip
scores and features, and a notebook that regenerates every statistic in this paper from the released
CSVs.


2   Related work

Controllable music generation spans text conditioning [7–9] and signal-level controls [10]; recent
work adds continuous valence–arousal conditioning precisely because text-based emotional prompting
is considered unreliable [11]. Our audit quantifies that assumed-weak baseline as graded curves.
Adherence evaluation typically reports CLAP-style similarity between prompt and output [1].
Closest to our question, AImoclips [12] finds human-rated emotion conveyance biased toward
neutrality; we find the objective analogue (clustered “house levels,” narrow range) with graded
ladders, phrasing contrasts, and an acoustic mechanism account that human ratings cannot provide.
Music emotion recognition supplies the measurement substrate: continuous arousal annotations
[13, 14] and evidence that arousal is the stable, acoustically grounded affect axis [3].


3   Materials and method

Models. Five open checkpoints chosen to cross scale, era, and architecture: MusicGen-small and
MusicGen-medium (2023; autoregressive LM over EnCodec tokens; scale axis held within family)
[7]; Stable Audio Open 1.0 (2024; latent diffusion) [8]; Stable Audio 3 Small Music (2026; music-
tuned latent diffusion; era axis held within vendor) [15]; and ACE-Step 1.5 turbo (2026; hybrid
LM-planner+DiT, RL-tuned for prompt adherence) [9]. We abbreviate mgs, mgm, sao, sa3s, ace.
Commercial systems are excluded on license-risk grounds; the roster is open-weights only by design.
Prompt matrix (frozen before generation). Five genre palettes (rock, EDM, hip-hop, acoustic
folk, orchestral) × five requested levels L ∈ {1, 3, 5, 7, 9} × two phrasings: numeric (“instrumental
genre track, energy level L out of 11”) and verbal (“instrumental genre track, descriptor” with the
fixed ladder very calm / calm / moderate energy / energetic / extremely intense). “Instrumental” is
prepended throughout to neutralize the vocal-capability confound (some rosters cannot sing). Six
seeds per cell give 5×50×6 = 1,500 clips of ∼30 s.
Instruments. All clips pass through one front end (mono sum, 22.05 kHz, first 30 s [16]) into
three scorers. (i) The Arousal Index [4]: a ridge regression over six descriptors of three named
features—loudness (frame RMS), noisiness (zero-crossing rate), and a tonality index (−10 log10
spectral flatness), each as mean and standard deviation—trained on DEAM arousal annotations [13]
(R2 =0.29, 5-fold), transferring to PMEmo [14] at Spearman ρ=0.77, and isotonically calibrated
(out-of-fold) to an absolute 0–11 scale. Its known limitation is inherited honestly: DEAM under-
represents arousal extremes, so the calibrated scale compresses above ∼6.5; we therefore also report
pre-calibration (“raw”) scores where the top of the scale matters (§4.4). (ii) Integrated loudness in
LUFS [5, 17]. (iii) CLAP cosine similarity between each clip and its own prompt [6]. Normalized
lens: every clip is additionally loudness-normalized to −14 LUFS and re-scored by (i) and (iii);
compliance that survives normalization reflects changed musical content, not gain.
Positive controls. Real DEAM excerpts of known low vs. high arousal run through the identical
stack score 3.7–4.1 vs. 6.0–6.5, confirming the instruments separate genuine arousal differences in
this pipeline before any claim is made about generated audio.

Statistics. Pre-committed before scoring: per model×phrasing, the median score per level; mono-
tonicity as Spearman ρ(level, score); dynamic range as the level-9 minus level-1 difference; the
phrasing gap; and the normalized-lens decomposition—each with 2,000-resample clip-level bootstrap

informative (§4.1). The genre-band analysis uses energy bands fixed before scoring for the four
instrumentation palettes; the rock band is analyst-set from a documented prior taxonomy and flagged
as such, with a rock-excluded sensitivity check.


4     Results

4.1    Numbers do nothing; words work—narrowly

Table 1 gives the headline. Under numeric instructions, no model moves: all Spearman CIs cover zero
and every curve sits at a model-specific house level (numeric mean 6.04 sao, 5.83 mgm, 5.81 mgs,
5.80 sa3s, 4.35 ace)—an objective counterpart to the “bias toward neutrality” reported for categorical
emotion intents [12]. Under verbal instructions every model becomes reliably monotonic (ρ from .33
to .54, all CIs excluding zero), and the verbal−numeric contrast ∆ρ excludes zero for all five models.
The instruction format is not cosmetic: these systems parse “extremely intense” but not “energy level
9 out of 11.”
Achieved range is another matter. The pre-registered median movement from “very calm” to
“extremely intense” spans 0.10 (ace) to 2.05 (mgs) points of an 11-point scale—at best ∼19% of the
scale. Mean-based deltas are systematically smaller for the mid-pack (mgs 0.93 vs. median 2.05; sa3s
0.83 vs. 1.60): the achieved-movement distribution is right-skewed, i.e., failure is clip-concentrated—
a minority of generations ignore the instruction and drag the mean; the typical clip complies more
than the mean suggests. For ace, neither median nor mean excludes zero: the 2026 adherence-tuned
model has no established verbal energy range on this audit, in the direction opposite its design claim
[9].

4.2   Mechanism: mostly the music, partly the volume knob

Two independent decompositions agree (Table 2). First, the normalized lens: the content share—the
fraction of each model’s verbal ∆ that survives −14 LUFS normalization—is .76 (mgm), .76 (mgs),
.82 (sa3s), and .59 (sao); ace’s ratio is undefined (its raw ∆ CI covers zero: no reliable compliance
to decompose). Second, the feature attribution: because the Arousal Index is linear in standardized
descriptors, each model’s pre-calibration score delta splits exactly into loudness, noisiness, and
tonality cluster contributions. Noisiness and tonality carry the movement for every complying model;
loudness contributes at most 38% (sao) and essentially nothing for mgm (−2%). The consistency
check lands: sao’s feature-level loudness share (38%) matches its normalization-vanishing share
(1 − .59 = 41%) via independent computations. Verbal LUFS deltas tell the same story from a
third angle: sao raises loudness by +6.3 dB from “very calm” to “extremely intense”—an order of
magnitude in power—while ace’s peak-normalizing decoder clamps loudness entirely (−0.4 dB),
a design property we report as behavior, not confound. Practitioner reading: “adding energy”
predominantly means noisier, less tonal music; sao also leans on gain, which a mastering chain would
erase.

4.3   Compliance is gated by genre plausibility

Requested levels were graded against per-genre plausible energy bands fixed before scoring (folk
0.5–5.5, EDM 4–8, hip-hop 2–7, orchestral 3–7; rock 5–8, analyst-set). Per-unit achieved movement
concentrates on steps that stay inside the band: pooled in-band gain +0.223 per requested unit vs.
+0.033 out-of-band (difference +0.190, CI [+0.096, +0.279]; per-model differences significant for
ace +0.297, sa3s +0.291, mgm +0.206; not for mgs +0.132 or sao +0.026). The pattern strengthens
slightly when the analyst-set rock band is excluded (pooled difference +0.217, CI [+0.111, +0.321]).
Notably, the model with the largest volume-knob share (sao) is the one whose compliance is not
band-gated—gain applies anywhere; content change is constrained by the palette. We report this as
association, not causation. A genre-controlled check agrees: on a fixed-anchor CLAP axis whose
anchors share each genre phrase, verbal moves every model and numeric stays flat—not cross-genre
acoustics. Failure has direction: rock generations start near each model’s ceiling even at “very calm”
(mgm rock level-1 mean 6.37; sa3s 6.48)—these systems cannot make calm rock—while folk fails
upward, plateauing at 4–6 under “extremely intense.”

4.4   The top of the scale: saturation vs. instrument ceiling

Three of five models score lower at level 9 than 7 under verbal instruction—model behavior, or
our instrument’s top-end compression? Three witnesses adjudicate. For ace the drop is real on all
three: calibrated Arousal Index −0.55 [-0.82, -0.25], own-prompt CLAP −0.086 [-0.138, -0.031], and
pre-calibration score −0.37 [-0.56, -0.18]—pushed to “extremely intense,” ace produces audio that
even matches its own prompt less. For mgs the calibrated drop is ambiguous (−0.42 [-0.86, +0.03])
precisely because calibration compresses the top of the scale, but the pre-calibration score drops
clearly (−0.73 [-1.37, -0.15], CLAP marginal at −0.085 [-0.170, +0.004]): real saturation, masked
by the instrument ceiling. For sa3s no witness establishes a drop. mgm and sao rise on all witnesses.
The same pre-calibration lens quantifies how much the ceiling understates top-end movement (e.g.,
mgm verbal ∆raw =1.86 [1.47, 2.24] vs. calibrated 1.28)—a caution for calibrated-instrument audits
of generated audio. A full raw-scale replicate of Table 1 (released) preserves the ordering (ρ from

4.5   Scale, era, and the frontier

Within family, the larger MusicGen is more monotonic in point estimate (.54 vs. .39; ∆ρ +.15
[-.00, +.32], not established). Within vendor, two years (sao→sa3s) improved musicality by ear while
the compliance point estimate fell (.50 → .38; ∆ρ −.12 [-.27, +.02]): no decline established, but
the interval nearly excludes any gain—better music, not better compliance. The 2026 adherence-
RL-tuned frontier model (ace), as configured here, is the weakest complier on every estimate, with
a genre-specific collapse (EDM per-genre ρ=.08 vs. rock .71) and confirmed top-level saturation.
A pre-registered directional hypothesis—rock shows lower verbal elasticity than instrumentation
palettes—held for three of five models (rock-minus-others ρ: mgm −.21, sa3s −.27, sao −.20; mgs
null; ace reversed via its EDM collapse); the edge-cell table reframes it as a ceiling: rock cannot go
down.

5     Limitations

Our arousal instrument transfers from human-annotated music to generated audio; positive controls
confirm it separates real arousal, three-instrument convergence carries the headline claims, but transfer
error cannot be fully excluded—hence triangulated top-of-scale claims and reported pre-calibration
scores. The calibrated scale compresses above ∼6.5. Own-prompt CLAP compares each clip to
a different anchor text per level; a released fixed-anchor CLAP axis (genre-matched calm/intense
anchors, clap-htsat-unfused, deterministic) replicates the contrast: all five verbal ρ CIs exclude
zero (.27–.60); numeric flat. CLAP inference was not bit-reproducible across runs (cell-mean drift
≤ 0.033; Arousal Index and LUFS reproduce exactly); all CLAP verdicts are stable across observed
draws; released values are from the canonical run. Six seeds per cell bound precision (hence CIs
everywhere); clips are single 30-s windows; prompts are English; five genre palettes; open models
only; no listener study—human benchmarks [12] are complementary evidence.

6     Conclusion

Deployed text-to-music systems take perceptual direction only in words, move a fraction of the
requested range, mostly change the music rather than the volume—except where they don’t,
quantifiably—and comply mainly where the genre palette makes the request plausible. A calibrated,
interpretable instrument makes each claim checkable: its weights decompose compliance. Prompts,
scores, features, and the statistics notebook are released at https://github.com/ConnerFarrell/
energy-audit.

Responsible use statement. This work audits open-weights music generators with open data;
findings are aggregate measurements, not product judgments, and the released multi-instrument stack
exposes single-metric gaming. Audio exhibits respect each model’s license (MusicGen CC-BY-NC;
Stability attribution; ACE-Step MIT); no human subjects or personal data are involved. Residual
risks—steering generation toward manipulative affect, or misreading the instrument as a general
emotion proxy—are mitigated by releasing the full stack, its negative results, and calibration limits.

C    Reproducibility
Corpus: 1,500 clips, ∼30 s, six fixed seeds per cell. Scoring front end identical for all models
(mono, 22.05 kHz, 30 s). All bootstrap CIs: 2,000 clip-level resamples, percentile method, fixed
seed; every statistic in the paper regenerates digit-for-digit from the released scored_full_v2.csv
(per-clip scores, both lenses), features_verbal.csv (per-clip descriptors), and the s6_*.csv and
s7_*.csv banks via the released notebook. Model checkpoints and exact revisions are listed in the
repository.
